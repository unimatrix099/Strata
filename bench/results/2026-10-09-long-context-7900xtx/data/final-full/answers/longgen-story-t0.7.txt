# The Keeper of the Edge

## Chapter 1: January – The Weight of Stone

The wind did not howl; it screamed. It was a high, thin shriek that tore through the gaps in the granite walls of Blackwood Point, finding the cracks in the mortar, the loose shingles on the roof, and the single pane of glass in the keeper’s cottage window. It was January, and the world beyond the island’s jagged coastline was a blur of gray mist and churning, iron-colored water.

Elias Thorne stood on the gallery railing, his back to the sea, facing the lens. He was fifty-four years old, with hands that looked like they had been carved from the same basalt as the island itself—rough, dark, and unyielding. His face was a map of weathered lines, deepened by decades of squinting into the glare of the sun and the salt spray of the Atlantic. He adjusted the brass key in his pocket, feeling the cold metal against his thigh, a tactile anchor in a world that felt increasingly untethered.

Inside the lantern room, the Fresnel lens sat in its bath of mercury, a giant glass eye staring out into the void. It rotated on its clockwork mechanism, a rhythmic *whir-click, whir-click* that had become the heartbeat of Elias’s life. The light swept out, a beam of pure white fire cutting through the fog, illuminating nothing but the swirling snow and the distant, shadowy shapes of rocks that had claimed more than a few ships over the last century.

Elias checked the oil reservoir. It was full. The wick was trimmed. The temperature in the room was thirty degrees, held steady by the small electric heater he had installed two winters ago, replacing the old kerosene stove that had once threatened to singe his eyebrows off. He preferred the electric hum; it was cleaner, quieter, less prone to the erratic flickers of flame that could cause a keeper to misread the rhythm of the rotation.

He descended the spiral staircase, his boots finding the worn grooves in the cast-iron steps. The stairs were cold, radiating a chill that seeped through the soles of his boots and settled in his bones. As he reached the ground floor, the noise of the wind seemed to drop away, replaced by the low, resonant thrum of the ocean battering the base of the tower.

In the kitchen, the silence was absolute. There was no television, no radio, no internet. There was only the ticking of the grandfather clock in the corner and the occasional creak of the house settling under the pressure of the gale. Elias moved to the stove, where a pot of stew had been simmering since morning. He stirred it, the wooden spoon scraping against the enamel. The aroma of rosemary, thyme, and beef broth filled the small room, a comforting scent that clashed violently with the sterile cold outside.

He set a single plate on the table. One fork. One knife. One glass of water.

Elias sat down. He did not eat immediately. He listened.

He listened to the wind trying to peel the roof off the cottage. He listened to the waves crashing against the cliffs, a sound so powerful it vibrated in his chest cavity. He listened to the silence between the gusts, a brief, terrifying pause where the island held its breath.

The supply boat was due in three days. It was the only link to the mainland, the only thread connecting him to the rest of humanity. The Coast Guard vessel, the *USCGC Margaret*, came once a month, weather permitting. In January, "weather permitting" was a polite fiction. Often, the boat simply didn’t come. The sea was too rough, the winds too strong. Elias had been cut off before. He had rationed his tinned goods for two months. He had eaten seaweed and dried fish, things he could gather from the rocks. He had learned to make his own soap from rendered fat and ash. He had learned that loneliness was not a feeling, but a physical weight, pressing down on the shoulders, tightening the throat.

He took a spoonful of stew. It was hot, savory, and grounding. He ate slowly, deliberately. Every bite was a victory against the entropy of the island.

When he finished, he washed the plate. He dried it. He placed it back in the cabinet. He wiped the table. He checked the windows. He checked the lighthouse logbook. He wrote the time, the temperature, the wind speed, the visibility.

*08:45. Temp: 28°F. Wind: 40mph NW. Vis: Poor. Fog dense.*

He closed the book. He picked up his coat. He went back out into the storm.

***

The walk from the cottage to the base of the tower was a journey across a landscape of desolation. The island was small, barely a mile long and half a mile wide, a jagged tooth of rock jutting out of the Atlantic. There were no trees, only scrubby grasses that had been beaten flat by the wind, growing in clumps behind the shelter of large boulders. The soil was thin, acidic, and prone to erosion.

Elias walked with his head down, his collar turned up against the spray. The wind pushed against him, a physical hand trying to shove him off the cliff’s edge. He moved carefully, testing each step before committing his weight. The rocks were slick with ice and sea spray. One slip meant a fall down the sheer drop to the sea below, where the currents would drag a body out to the deep.

He reached the base of the tower and entered the heavy iron door. The interior was warmer here, shielded by the thick stone walls. He climbed the stairs again, not to the lantern room, but to the lower levels, where the machinery lived.

The lighthouse was a marvel of engineering, a testament to a time when light was the primary defense against disaster. The clockwork mechanism, a series of gears and weights, drove the rotation of the lens. It required winding every twelve hours. Elias checked the tension in the cables. He oiled the joints. He inspected the glass lenses for cracks or sea spray buildup. It was a ritual, a prayer performed in grease and brass.

While he worked, his mind wandered. He thought about the last time he had seen another human face. It had been six months ago, when the supply boat came during a rare calm spell. The captain, a young woman named Sarah, had handed over crates of food and a box of mail. They had stood on the dock for ten minutes, talking about nothing important. The weather. The tide. The price of coffee in Boston.

She had looked at him with a mixture of curiosity and pity. Elias had seen it before. People looked at lighthouse keepers as if they were monks, or hermits, or madmen. They projected their own fears onto him. *How do you stand it?* they asked. *Aren’t you afraid?*

Elias was not afraid. He was afraid of forgetting. Not the names of people, not the faces of his parents, but the sensation of connection. The feeling of being seen. The warmth of a hand on his shoulder. The sound of a voice calling his name.

He finished the maintenance. He wiped his hands on a rag. He returned to the lantern room. The light was sweeping out, steady and rhythmic. *Flash... pause... flash... pause.* It was a Morse code of survival. *I am here. I am watching. I am safe.*

He sat in the chair by the window, watching the light dance across the fog. The beam illuminated the swirling moisture, creating tunnels of light that seemed to lead into infinity. He closed his eyes. He let the rhythm of the light sync with his breathing. In. Out. Flash. Pause.

For a moment, he was not alone. He was part of the machine. He was part of the light. He was part of the sea.

***

Later that night, the storm intensified. The waves rose higher, crashing against the cliff with explosive force. The house shook. A window cracked in the bedroom, a spiderweb fracture across the glass. Elias woke up, his heart pounding. He lay still, listening to the wind tear at the siding.

He got up. He put on his boots. He went to the tool shed. He found a sheet of plywood and some nails. He went back to the cottage. He boarded up the cracked window, hammering the nails through the wood, sealing the breach. The wind screamed against the new barrier, but it held.

He returned to bed. He lay awake for hours, listening to the house groan. He thought about the ships out there. The trawlers, the cargo vessels, the private yachts. They were navigating by star and compass, by GPS, by radar. But in the worst of it, in the fog and the dark, they still looked for the flash. They still trusted the keeper.

It was a strange responsibility. To be invisible, yet essential. To be forgotten, yet relied upon.

At dawn, the wind dropped. The sea was still rough, but the screaming had stopped. Elias went out to check the exterior. The plywood was wet, sagging slightly, but intact. He went to the supply shed. He counted his stores. Tinned beans. Tinned soup. Rice. Pasta. Flour. Sugar. Oil. He had enough for two months. Maybe three, if he was careful.

He went to the well. He pumped water. He filled a bucket. He brought it to the house. He boiled it. He made tea.

He sat on the porch, wrapped in a blanket, watching the sun try to break through the clouds. It was a pale, weak disc, offering little heat. But it was there.

He thought about the mail. He hadn’t received any in six months. He wondered if anyone was still writing to him. His sister, Martha, in Ohio. His old friend, David, in Portland. Did they remember him? Did they think he was still there, tending the flame?

He went inside. He opened the logbook. He wrote the new entry.

*06:15. Temp: 32°F. Wind: 15mph NW. Vis: Improving. Fog clearing.*

He looked at the page. The handwriting was neat, consistent. It was the only thing in his life that was predictable.

***

The next day, the sky cleared. The sea calmed. The sun rose, casting a golden light over the gray rocks. Elias walked the perimeter of the island. He checked the bird colonies. The puffins had left for the winter, but the gulls remained, scavenging on the cliffs. He checked the vegetation. The grass was brown, brittle. He checked the soil. It was frozen hard.

He found a piece of driftwood on the beach. It was large, bleached white by the sun and salt. He dragged it to the shed. He would use it for fuel. Every scrap was precious.

He spent the afternoon repairing a fence post that had been broken by the wind. He used a spade and a wrench. He worked until his muscles ached. He liked the ache. It was a proof of life.

In the evening, he cooked a meal of rice and beans. He ate it while reading a book he had brought six months ago. It was a history of the Roman Empire. He had read it twice. He was reading it a third time. He liked the repetition. He liked knowing what would happen next. It was a comfort in a world that was constantly changing, constantly eroding.

He went to bed early. He slept deeply, dreamlessly.

***

Three days later, the supply boat arrived.

Elias saw it from the gallery. A small white dot against the gray horizon. He watched it grow, resolving into the shape of a Coast Guard cutter. He felt a surge of emotion he couldn’t name. Relief? Anxiety? Anticipation?

He went down to the dock. The boat maneuvered carefully into the cove, battling the residual currents. The captain, Sarah, stepped off. She was wearing a heavy coat, her face red from the cold.

"Elias!" she called, her voice carrying over the wind.

He waved. He didn’t speak. He just waved.

She walked up the path to the cottage. She carried a crate. She set it down on the porch.

"Good to see you," she said. She smiled, but it was a tight smile. "How are you?"

"I’m fine," Elias said. His voice was rough, unused. "How’s the sea?"

"Better than it was," Sarah said. "We had to fight our way in. The swells were high. We almost didn’t come."

"I know," Elias said. "I watched you."

Sarah looked at him. She studied his face, his hands, his clothes. She was checking for signs of decay. For signs of madness.

"Any problems?" she asked.

"No," Elias said. "House is fine. Lens is fine. Machinery is fine."

"Food?"

"Enough," Elias said. "I rationed."

Sarah nodded. She opened the crate. She took out boxes. Canned fruit. Fresh vegetables—apples, carrots, potatoes. Bread. Cheese. Milk. Chocolate. Coffee.

"Here’s the mail," she said, handing him a bundle of letters.

Elias took them. He held them against his chest. He didn’t open them yet. He wanted to savor the moment. The physical weight of connection.

"Anything else?" Sarah asked.

"No," Elias said.

Sarah paused. She looked at the lighthouse. She looked at the sea.

"It’s hard," she said. "Being out here."

"It’s quiet," Elias said.

"Quiet can be loud," Sarah said.

Elias looked at her. He wanted to say something profound. Something about the nature of solitude. About the beauty of the empty space. But he didn’t have the words. He had lost the ability to speak in long, flowing sentences. His language had become simple, functional.

"Thank you," he said.

Sarah nodded. She picked up the empty crate.

"I have to go," she said. "Tide’s turning."

"Okay," Elias said.

She walked back to the boat. Elias watched her go. He watched the boat leave. He watched the white dot disappear into the mist.

He went inside. He sat at the table. He opened the mail.

There was a letter from Martha. *How are you? Are you eating well? I worry.*

There was a letter from David. *Happy New Year. How’s the light? Do you see the stars?*

There was a postcard from a stranger. *I saw your light from my boat. It’s beautiful. Keep it burning.*

Elias read them. He read them twice. He folded them. He put them in a drawer.

He made coffee. He drank it slowly. He listened to the silence. It was different now. It was not empty. It was filled with the echoes of the voices in the letters.

He went out to the gallery. The sun was setting. The sky was a canvas of purple and orange. The sea was calm, a sheet of glass.

He lit the lamp. The beam swept out.

He stood there for a long time, watching the light travel across the water. He felt a strange sense of purpose. He was not just keeping a light. He was keeping a promise. A promise to the sea. A promise to the sky. A promise to the people who would never know his name.

He turned off the light when the sun rose. He went to bed. He slept well.

***

The rest of January was uneventful. The weather held. The sea remained calm. Elias ate the fresh food. He felt stronger. He felt lighter. The weight of the loneliness seemed to lift, just a little.

He walked the island. He explored the north side, a place he rarely went. It was a sheer cliff, facing the open ocean. The wind here was constant, a steady push. He found a cave, a small hollow in the rock. He stepped inside. It was dry. The wind was blocked. He sat on a stone. He looked out at the sea.

He thought about the cave. It was a shelter. A refuge. He thought about building a fire in it. He thought about sleeping there. He rejected the idea. He was a keeper. He belonged in the house.

He returned to the tower. He wound the clockwork. He cleaned the lens. He wrote in the log.

*January 31. Temp: 35°F. Wind: 5mph W. Vis: Clear. Moon: Full.*

He looked at the moon. It was huge, white, hanging low over the horizon. The light from the lighthouse and the light from the moon merged, creating a double glow on the water.

He felt a sudden urge to speak. To say something out loud. To break the silence.

"Hello," he said.

The word hung in the air. It sounded strange, foreign.

"Hello," he said again.

He smiled. It was a small, private smile. He went inside. He made tea. He read his book. He went to bed.

***

## Chapter 2: February – The Ice

February arrived with a cold that seemed to come from the earth itself. The air was dry, biting. The sea froze at the edges of the shore, forming jagged shards of ice that clattered against the rocks. The island was silent, muffled by the snow.

The supply boat did not come. The weather was too severe. Elias was alone.

He checked his stores. He had enough for another month. He began to ration. He ate less. He drank more water. He exercised, doing push-ups and squats in the kitchen, trying to keep his body active. He wanted to stay strong. He wanted to be ready for the spring.

The cold penetrated the house. The heating was electric, but the insulation was poor. Elias wore his coat indoors. He wrapped himself in blankets. He slept in his clothes.

He spent hours in the lantern room. The cold was easier to bear there, surrounded by the warmth of the machine. He watched the light rotate. He listened to the gears. He thought about the mechanics of the universe. The rotation of the earth. The orbit of the moon. The cycle of the seasons. Everything moved. Everything changed. Even the stone. Even the sea.

He began to talk to the lens.

"Good morning," he said to it.

The lens did not answer. It just rotated.

"Nice day," he said.

The light swept out, illuminating the snow.

Elias felt a strange comfort in the routine. The lens was a companion. It was silent, but it was present. It was a constant. In a world of chaos, it was order.

He started to keep a second log. Not for the Coast Guard, but for himself. He wrote down his thoughts. His dreams. His memories.

*February 4. I dreamed of my mother. She was making bread. The kitchen was warm. She smelled like yeast and soap. I woke up and the room was cold. I cried. I don’t know why. Maybe because I forgot the smell.*

*February 10. The ice is thick. I saw a seal on the beach. It looked at me. It had eyes like my dog, Buster. Buster died ten years ago. I miss him. I miss him every day.*

*February 15. I saw a ship. It was far out. It flashed its light. It was a distress signal? Or just navigation? I watched it for an hour. It moved on. I felt relieved. I don’t want to be needed. I want to be left alone.*

He wrote these things in a small notebook he had found in the attic. He hid it in a box under the bed. It was a secret. It was a record of his inner life, the life that no one else could see.

***

One night, the temperature dropped to -10°F. The pipes in the house froze. The water stopped flowing. Elias had to melt snow in the kitchen to boil for tea. He chopped wood for the emergency stove, a small kerosene heater he kept in the shed.

He built a fire in the stove. The heat was weak, but it was something. He sat by it, warming his hands. He watched the flames. They danced, flickering, alive.

He thought about fire. It was the oldest technology. It was the first light. It was the first tool. It was the thing that kept humans alive in the dark.

The lighthouse was a fire. A giant, electric fire. It was the modern version of the ancient flame.

He felt a connection to the keepers who had come before him. Men who had died in these walls. Men who had gone mad. Men who had jumped from the gallery.

He looked at the window. The glass was frosted. He could not see out. He felt trapped. The world had shrunk to the size of the room.

He stood up. He went to the door. He opened it. The cold hit him like a wall. He stepped out.

He walked to the tower. He climbed the stairs. He entered the lantern room.

He looked out. The snow was falling, heavy and fast. The light was a beam of white in a world of white. It was almost invisible.

He felt a surge of panic. What if the light failed? What if the fog was too thick? What if the storm was too strong?

He checked the mechanism. It was working. He checked the oil. It was full. He checked the wick. It was lit.

He breathed. He calmed himself.

He went back down. He went to the stove. He sat down. He watched the fire.

He whispered to the fire. "Stay lit," he said. "Stay lit."

The fire crackled. It did not go out.

***

The days blurred. Time lost its meaning. There was no sun. There was no moon. There was only snow.

Elias stopped writing in the logbook for the Coast Guard. He stopped winding the clockwork as often. He let the weights run down. He let the rotation slow.

He sat in the chair. He stared at the wall. He thought about nothing. He thought about everything.

He thought about his life. He had been a sailor. He had been a fisherman. He had been a soldier. He had been a husband. He had been a father. He had been a keeper.

He had lost the wife. She had left him, taking the children. He had lost the job. He had lost the friends. He had lost the connection.

He had found the island. He had found the light.

Was it a refuge? Or was it a prison?

He didn’t know. He didn’t care.

***

A week later, the snow stopped. The sun returned. The ice melted. The sea opened up.

Elias went out. He walked the beach. He found tracks. Seal tracks. Bird tracks. Human tracks? No.

He found a message in a bottle. It was a joke, a prank. *Happy Birthday, Elias.* It was signed by a group of sailors from a nearby port.

Elias laughed. It was a rusty, creaking sound. He hadn’t laughed in months.

He put the bottle on the shelf. He kept it.

He felt a spark. A tiny ember of hope.

He went inside. He cleaned the house. He washed his clothes. He cooked a meal. He ate it. He felt human.

He went to the tower. He wound the clockwork. He cleaned the lens. He wrote in the log.

*February 28. Temp: 20°F. Wind: 10mph N. Vis: Clear. Snow: Gone.*

He looked at the page. He felt proud. He had survived.

***

## Chapter 3: March – The Thaw

March was a month of contradictions. It was cold and warm. It was gray and green. The ice retreated, revealing the brown earth beneath. Patches of snow remained, but the grass began to stir.

The supply boat came. The weather was mild. Sarah returned.

"Elias," she said. "You look better."

"I am better," Elias said.

"How?"

"I slept," Elias said. "I ate. I walked."

Sarah nodded. She handed over the mail. There were more letters. Martha. David. A new one from a school teacher in Maine who wanted Elias to write to her students. *Tell them about the light,* she had written. *Tell them about the sea.*

Elias read the letter. He felt a strange responsibility. To speak to children. To explain the world.

He went to the schoolhouse on the mainland? No. He could not leave. He wrote a letter.

*Dear Students,*

*My name is Elias. I live on an island. I keep the light. The light is there to warn you. To tell you where the rocks are. To tell you where the safe water is. The sea is dangerous. It is beautiful. It is indifferent. You must respect it.*

*Love, Elias.*

He put the letter in an envelope. He gave it to Sarah.

"Will this reach them?" Elias asked.

"Yes," Sarah said. "I’ll mail it."

Elias nodded. He watched her leave.

He felt a new purpose. He was not just a keeper. He was a teacher. A storyteller.

***

He began to explore the island more deeply. He went to the high ground, the central ridge. He looked out over the island. He saw the patterns of the land. The valleys. The cliffs. The coves.

He mapped it in his mind. He drew a map in the sand. He erased it. He drew it again.

He found a stream, a trickle of water that had been frozen for months. It was flowing now, clear and cold. He drank from it. It tasted sweet.

He found flowers. Small, hardy blooms that pushed through the snow. Crocuses. Snowdrops. He touched them gently. He marveled at their resilience.

He thought about his own resilience. He was like the flowers. Small. Hard. Persistent.

He began to garden. He cleared a small plot near the cottage. He planted seeds. Radishes. Lettuce. Herbs. It was a gamble. The soil was poor. The wind was strong. But he tried.

He watered them. He protected them with stones. He watched them grow.

He felt a connection to the earth. To the cycle of life. To the spring.

***

The sea was calm. The fog was gone. The sky was blue.

Elias spent hours on the gallery. He watched the ships. He watched the birds. He watched the clouds.

He felt a sense of peace. The loneliness was still there, but it was softer. It was a companion, not an enemy.

He talked to the lens.

"Good morning," he said.

The lens rotated.

"Hello," he said.

The light swept out.

He felt a sense of communion. He was part of the light. He was part of the island. He was part of the world.

He wrote in his private notebook.

*March 15. I am not alone. I am connected. To the light. To the sea. To the sky. To the people who read my letters. I am a node in a network. A point in a web. I am held.*

He smiled. He went inside. He made tea. He read his book. He went to bed.

***

## Chapter 4: April – The Storms

April brought the storms back. The wind returned, stronger than before. The sea churned. The rain fell in sheets.

The supply boat was delayed. Elias was alone again.

He worried about the garden. The rain would wash away the soil. The wind would break the plants. He covered them with tarps. He staked them. He tried to protect them.

He worried about the house. The roof was old. The shingles were missing. He patched them with tar and canvas. He boarded up the windows. He checked the drains. He cleared the gutters.

He worried about the tower. The wind was strong. The tower was tall. It swayed. He checked the foundation. He checked the bolts. He tightened the screws.

He worked hard. He sweated. He bled. He was tired.

He felt a sense of urgency. The island was attacking him. It was trying to break him.

He fought back. He repaired. He reinforced. He survived.

One night, the wind hit 60mph. The house shook. The windows rattled. The roof groaned.

Elias stood in the kitchen. He held onto the table. He watched the roof.

He heard a crash. A shingle flew off. It landed on the porch.

He ran outside. He secured the roof. He nailed down the loose pieces. He covered the hole with a tarp.

He went back inside. He was soaked. He shivered.

He built a fire. He warmed himself. He waited.

The storm lasted three days. Elias did not sleep. He watched. He listened. He worked.

On the fourth day, the storm broke. The wind dropped. The rain stopped.

Elias collapsed. He slept for twelve hours.

When he woke up, the world was different. The air was fresh. The sky was clear. The sea was calm.

He went outside. He checked the damage. The garden was ruined. The plants were flattened. The soil was washed away.

He felt a pang of sadness. But he was not defeated. He was still here.

He went to the shed. He got new seeds. He replanted. He prepared the soil. He started again.

He wrote in the log.

*April 10. Temp: 40°F. Wind: 20mph S. Vis: Clear. Storm: Passed.*

He felt a sense of triumph. He had survived. He had endured.

***

## Chapter 5: May – The Blooming

May was the month of life. The island exploded with color. The grass turned green. The flowers bloomed. The birds returned.

The puffins came back. They gathered on the cliffs, their bright beaks flashing in the sun. The gulls shrieked. The terns dove.

Elias watched them. He felt a sense of joy. He was not alone. He was surrounded by life.

He walked the island. He saw the new growth. He saw the nests. He saw the eggs.

He felt a connection to the animals. He was part of the ecosystem. He was a predator. He was a protector. He was a observer.

He went to the garden. The new plants were growing. The radishes were sprouting. The lettuce was green. The herbs were fragrant.

He harvested some. He ate them fresh. They tasted like spring. They tasted like hope.

He went to the tower. He cleaned the lens. He polished the glass. He checked the mechanism.

He felt a sense of pride. The light was perfect. The machinery was perfect. The house was perfect.

He wrote in the log.

*May 20. Temp: 55°F. Wind: 10mph E. Vis: Clear. Flowers: Bloom.*

He looked at the page. He felt happy. He was alive.

***

## Chapter 6: June – The Heat

June brought the heat. The sun was strong. The air was thick. The sea was warm.

Elias felt a different kind of loneliness. It was a hot, sticky loneliness. It was a loneliness of excess. Too much sun. Too much light. Too much noise.

The gulls were loud. The insects were buzzing. The wind was hot.

He closed the windows. He stayed inside. He drank water. He ate light meals.

He went to the tower. He opened the vents. He let the air circulate. He checked the temperature of the lamp. It was hot. He adjusted the rotation.

He felt a sense of irritation. The island was too alive. It was too loud. He wanted silence.

He went to the cave. He sat in the shade. He watched the sea.

He thought about the heat. It was a test. A test of endurance. A test of patience.

He endured. He waited.

He wrote in his private notebook.

*June 15. The heat is a burden. It weighs on the mind. It makes the thoughts slow. It makes the body heavy. I want rain. I want cold. I want quiet.*

He felt a sense of restlessness. He was trapped in the heat. He was trapped in the island.

***

## Chapter 7: July – The Calm

July was calm. The wind died. The sea became glass. The sun was steady.

Elias felt a sense of peace. The heat was less oppressive. The air was cooler at night.

He walked the beach at night. He watched the stars. They were bright, clear, unobstructed. He saw the constellations. He saw the planets. He saw the Milky Way.

He felt small. He was a speck in a vast universe. He was a dot on a map.

But he was there. He was watching. He was keeping the light.

He thought about the ships. They were far away. They were safe. They were guided by his light.

He felt a sense of purpose. He was useful. He was needed.

He went to the garden. He watered the plants. He picked tomatoes. He picked peppers. He picked herbs.

He cooked a meal. He ate it on the porch. He watched the stars.

He felt a sense of gratitude. He was alive. He was well. He was safe.

He wrote in the log.

*July 10. Temp: 70°F. Wind: 5mph W. Vis: Perfect. Stars: Bright.*

He looked at the page. He felt content.

***

## Chapter 8: August – The Storms

August brought the storms back. The hurricanes. The typhoons. The winds.

The sky turned black. The sea rose. The waves crashed.

Elias prepared. He secured the house. He secured the tower. He secured the garden.

He boarded up the windows. He filled the sandbags. He checked the supplies.

He went to the tower. He checked the lens. He checked the mechanism.

He waited.

The storm hit. It was violent. It was destructive. It was terrifying.

Elias stood in the kitchen. He watched the roof. He watched the walls. He watched the sea.

He felt fear. He felt anger. He felt determination.

He fought the storm. He repaired the damage. He replaced the shingles. He patched the walls.

He survived.

The storm lasted two days. Elias did not sleep. He worked. He watched. He endured.

On the third day, the storm broke. The wind dropped. The sea calmed.

Elias collapsed. He slept.

When he woke up, the world was damaged. The garden was destroyed. The trees were broken. The beach was changed.

But the house was standing. The tower was standing. The light was burning.

He felt a sense of pride. He had survived.

He wrote in the log.

*August 25. Temp: 65°F. Wind: 10mph N. Vis: Clear. Storm: Passed. Damage: Moderate.*

He felt a sense of resilience. He was strong. He was capable.

***
