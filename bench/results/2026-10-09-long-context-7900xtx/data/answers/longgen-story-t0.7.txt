# The Keeper of the Grey Stone

## Prologue: The Weight of Silence

The sea does not speak; it merely breathes. It inhales with the tide, pulling back to reveal the jagged teeth of the rock, and exhales with the storm, crashing against the granite cliffs with a fury that shakes the bones of the earth. For Elias Thorne, this rhythm was the only clock that mattered. It was a rhythm older than calendars, older than governments, older than the concept of time itself.

He stood on the balcony of the lighthouse, the iron railing cold beneath his gloved hands. The wind was a physical presence here, a heavy, wet blanket that pressed against his chest and tried to drag him into the abyss below. Below him, the ocean churned, a chaotic swirl of slate-grey and white foam, angry and indifferent. Above him, the sky was a bruised purple, threatening snow, threatening ice, threatening the kind of isolation that felt less like solitude and more like burial.

Elias was forty-two years old. He had come to the Isle of Crows three months ago, fleeing a life that had become too loud, too bright, and too fast. He had been an architect in London, designing glass towers that reflected the sun and hid the wind. He had been married for ten years, divorced for five. He had children who lived in cities he no longer visited, calling him only on birthdays with the perfunctory politeness of distant relatives. He had chosen the lighthouse not because he loved the sea, but because he feared the silence of his own apartment, a silence that had grown so loud it had started to crack the walls of his mind.

The Isle of Crows was not a place for tourists. It was a jagged splinter of rock in the North Atlantic, halfway between the Scottish mainland and the Faroe Islands. It was accessible only by a supply boat that came once a month, weather permitting. The lighthouse itself was a relic of the late 19th century, a tower of grey stone built by men who believed that light was a moral imperative, that darkness was a sin to be fought with oil and glass.

Elias had signed a contract for one year. One year to watch the light. One year to keep the beacon burning. One year to see if a man could survive without being seen.

He turned away from the railing and walked back inside. The door to the lantern room was heavy, oak and iron, sealed against the wind. As he closed it, the roar of the sea was muffled to a dull thrum, a heartbeat in the walls. The interior of the tower was circular, winding up in a spiral staircase that smelled of damp stone, old oil, and dust. The air was thin and cold, carrying the scent of the sea even when the doors were shut.

He climbed the stairs, his boots clicking against the iron treads. At the top, the lantern room was a cathedral of glass and metal. The lens was a masterpiece of optics, a massive Fresnel lens that bent light into a powerful beam, cutting through the fog and the night with surgical precision. It rotated slowly, driven by a clockwork mechanism that Elias wound every morning. The light was warm, golden, and steady. It was the only thing on the island that felt alive.

Elias checked the mechanism. The weights were descending, the gears clicking with a rhythmic precision that soothed him. He adjusted the oil lamp, trimming the wick, ensuring the flame was steady. He watched the light sweep across the darkness outside, a sweeping arc of gold that vanished into the mist and returned moments later. It was a hypnotic dance, a promise of safety to ships that might never come.

He descended the stairs, his legs heavy with the climb. The keeper’s quarters were on the third floor, a small, circular room with a window facing north, toward the open sea. The furniture was sparse: a narrow bed, a desk, a chair, a stove, and shelves lined with books. The books were mostly about lighthouses, about the sea, about the history of navigation. He had brought them with him, thinking they would be companions. They were not. They were echoes.

He sat at the desk and opened his journal. The ink was black, the paper rough. He wrote the date. He wrote the weather. He wrote the condition of the light. These were the requirements of his job. But beneath the factual entries, there was a blank space, a void where he was supposed to write about his feelings, his thoughts, his memories. He left it blank. He had come here to escape his memories, not to confront them.

The radio crackled on the shelf. It was an old device, shortwave, capable of reaching the mainland only on clear days. Elias did not use it often. It was a link to the world he had left behind, and he did not want to be linked. But sometimes, in the quiet hours of the night, he would turn it on, just to hear the static, the white noise of the universe. It was a sound that reminded him that he was not entirely alone, that there were other people, other lives, other lights burning somewhere in the dark.

Tonight, the radio was silent. The static was absent. The storm had swallowed the signals. Elias sat in the chair, listening to the wind howl against the glass. He thought of his daughter, Sarah. She was twenty-two, living in Edinburgh, studying law. He had not spoken to her in six months. The last time they had spoken, she had told him that he was disappearing. "You’re not living, Dad," she had said. "You’re just existing."

He had not answered. He had hung up the phone and packed his bag. He had chosen the Isle of Crows because it was a place where existing was the only option. You could not live on a rock in the sea. You could only survive. And survival, he thought, was a purer form of life than the complicated, messy existence of the mainland.

He made tea, using a tin kettle on the wood-burning stove. The fire crackled, the flames dancing in the iron grate. He drank the tea slowly, watching the steam rise, imagining it as a signal, a message to the ships. *I am here. I am watching. I am waiting.*

But who was he waiting for?

The night deepened. The light swept the darkness. Elias went to bed, pulling the heavy wool blankets up to his chin. He slept poorly, dreaming of glass towers shattering, of wind blowing through empty rooms, of a light that would not go out.

## Chapter 1: January – The First Month

January was a month of ice and iron. The sea froze in patches, slushy and cruel, clinging to the rocks like a disease. The wind was constant, a cold blade that cut through the stone walls of the lighthouse. Elias spent the first weeks of the year learning the rhythms of the island. He learned which rocks were dangerous, which paths were slippery, which corners of the tower trapped the cold.

He learned the schedule of the supply boat. It was due in late January, weather permitting. The boat was a small, battered vessel called *The Hope*, a name that Elias found ironic. It was a hope that was always fragile, always dependent on the whims of the weather. If the storms were bad, the boat would not come. If the boat did not come, Elias would be alone, with only his rations and his books and the light.

He rationed carefully. He had enough food for two months, but the winter was long, and the cold burned calories. He ate oatmeal, dried fish, potatoes, and canned stew. He grew herbs in a small box on the windowsill, trying to keep something green alive in the grey world. The mint and thyme were stubborn, fighting for light in the dim room.

One morning, Elias went out to check the exterior of the lighthouse. The wind was strong, pushing him against the wall. He wore a heavy coat, a scarf, and goggles. He looked down at the sea. It was rough, the waves breaking with white fury against the base of the tower. Spray flew up, misting his face with salt. He felt a strange exhilaration. The danger was real, immediate, and honest. There was no pretense here. The sea did not care if he was sad or happy. It only cared if he was steady.

He climbed down the side of the rock, using a ladder that was rusted and slippery. His boots slipped, and he caught himself on a jagged edge. He hung there for a moment, the wind pulling at his clothes, the sea roaring below. He felt the fear, sharp and electric. It was a good fear. It reminded him that he was alive.

He reached the landing, a small concrete pad at the base of the tower. He inspected the foundation, the iron bands that held the stone together. He checked the drainage, clearing away slush and ice. He worked with methodical precision, his hands numb, his mind clear. This was the work. This was the purpose. To keep the structure sound. To keep the light burning.

When he returned to the tower, he was shaking with cold. He stripped off his wet clothes and climbed into the bed. He lay there for an hour, watching the light sweep the room, a golden pulse that matched his heartbeat. He thought of the ships that passed in the night. He imagined them, tiny specks of light moving across the dark water. They were anonymous. He did not know their names, their cargo, their destinations. They were just lights moving in the dark.

He wondered if he was like them. Was he just a light moving in the dark? Was he anonymous?

The supply boat came on the 28th of January. The weather had cleared, the sky a pale, washed-out blue. Elias watched from the balcony as the boat appeared on the horizon, a dark shape against the grey sea. He felt a surge of relief, but also a strange anxiety. The arrival of the boat meant the arrival of the world. It meant voices, news, letters, the intrusion of the mainland into his sanctuary.

He went down to the landing. The boat docked, and the skipper, a man named Callum, stepped off. Callum was a large man, red-faced, with a beard like a thicket. He was from the island of Lewis, a man who had spent his life on the sea. He looked at Elias with a mixture of pity and respect.

"You look thin, Elias," Callum said, handing him a crate of supplies.

"I’m fine," Elias said.

"You look thin," Callum repeated, not as a question, but as a statement. He handed him a second crate. "Fuel. Oil for the lamp. Food. Canned goods. Potatoes. Onions."

"Thank you," Elias said.

Callum paused. He looked at the lighthouse, at the tower rising into the sky. "The light looks good," he said. "It’s been steady."

"It’s the clockwork," Elias said. "I wind it every day."

"Good," Callum said. "They need that light. The fishing boats. The cargo ships. They need to know where the rock is."

"I know," Elias said.

Callum reached into his pocket and pulled out a bundle of letters. "Post," he said. "From the mainland."

Elias took the letters. He did not open them. He placed them on the crate, not wanting to break the seal of his isolation. He did not want to know what was happening in London. He did not want to know about Sarah, or his ex-wife, or the buildings he had designed. He wanted to keep the letters as potential threats, unopened, unspoken.

Callum looked at him. "You got any news you want to send out?"

"No," Elias said. "Nothing."

Callum nodded. "Alright. I’ll be back in February. If the weather’s bad, I’ll be back in March. Don’t run out of oil."

"I won’t," Elias said.

Callum boarded the boat. The engine sputtered, then roared to life. The boat pulled away, cutting through the waves, shrinking to a dot on the horizon. Elias watched it go until it disappeared. Then he turned back to the tower, carrying the crates. The weight of them was heavy, not just in pounds, but in meaning. They were a tether to the world, a reminder that he was not entirely cut off.

He unpacked the supplies. He stacked the canned food, the oil drums, the potatoes. He organized them with care, creating a system that would last him through the winter. He opened one letter, the one with the official seal of the lighthouse authority. It was a routine inspection report, confirming that his logs were in order, that the light was functioning correctly. He filed it away.

He did not open the other letters. He placed them in a drawer, beneath the journal. They were ghosts, waiting to be summoned.

That night, he cooked a meal of canned stew and potatoes. He ate slowly, watching the light. The storm had returned, the wind howling again, the sea churning. He felt a profound sense of peace. The world was chaotic, but the lighthouse was stable. The light was steady. He was steady.

He wrote in his journal. He described the arrival of the boat, the supplies, the weather. He did not write about his feelings. He did not write about the letters. He wrote about the oil, the wick, the rotation of the lens. He wrote about the mechanics of survival.

## Chapter 2: February – The Storm

February was a month of violence. The storms came in waves, relentless and punishing. The sea rose, swallowing the lower rocks, crashing against the tower with a force that shook the foundations. The wind screamed, a high-pitched wail that penetrated the stone walls, finding its way into the smallest cracks.

Elias spent the first week of February inside the tower, unable to go out. The wind was too strong, the waves too high. He monitored the light from the inside, watching the lens rotate, ensuring that the mechanism was not damaged by the shaking of the tower. He checked the oil, the wick, the glass. He polished the lens, removing the dust that had settled in the corners. He cleaned the windows, wiping away the salt spray that had accumulated on the exterior glass.

The storm lasted for days. The sky was a wall of grey, the sun a myth. Elias lived by the light. When the storm blocked the sun, the golden beam of the lighthouse became the only source of illumination in the world. It swept the room, a rhythmic pulse that marked the passage of time. He slept when the light dimmed, woke when it brightened. He ate when the light swept the table, stopped when it moved away.

On the third day of the storm, the radio crackled. It was a faint signal, broken by static, but it was there. A voice, distant and distorted, called out.

"Isle of Crows, this is the Coast Guard. Do you read?"

Elias turned up the volume. He spoke into the microphone, his voice rough from disuse. "I read. This is Elias Thorne, keeper of Isle of Crows."

The voice returned, clearer now. "Elias, we have a situation. A fishing vessel, the *Mary Jane*, is in distress. Engine failure. Drifting toward the rocks. We’re trying to guide them, but the visibility is poor. We need your light. Can you increase the intensity?"

"I can adjust the focus," Elias said. "I can send a stronger beam."

"Do it," the voice said. "They’re close. They’re struggling to see the rocks. The light is their only guide."

Elias went to the lantern room. He adjusted the lens, narrowing the beam, concentrating the light into a sharper, more powerful arc. He watched the sea, looking for the *Mary Jane*. He saw nothing but the churning waves. But he kept the light steady, burning with intensity. He felt a strange connection to the ship, a thread of light that bound him to the sailors in the dark. He was not just keeping the light for the sake of the law. He was keeping it for the sake of the people on the *Mary Jane*. He was their guide, their hope, their eye in the storm.

He waited. He watched. He adjusted the beam, following the drift of the sea, anticipating where the ship might be. He imagined them, huddled in their cabin, watching for the sweep of the light. He imagined them praying, cursing, hoping. He felt their fear, their desperation. He felt it in his own chest, a tightness, a ache. He was not alone. He was connected to them by the light.

Hours passed. The storm raged. Elias did not eat. He did not sleep. He watched the light, adjusting it, guiding it. And then, on the edge of the horizon, he saw it. A flash of white, a flare. The *Mary Jane* had seen the light. They were turning, moving away from the rocks.

The radio crackled again. "Elias, they’re clear. They’re safe. Thank you. You saved them."

Elias slumped against the wall, the exhaustion hitting him like a physical blow. He closed his eyes, the light sweeping past him, a golden halo in the darkness. He had saved them. He had done his job. But the feeling was not triumphant. It was heavy. It was a reminder of the power of the light, the responsibility of the keeper. He was not just a caretaker. He was a savior. And saviors were lonely.

He went down to his quarters. He collapsed into the bed, the blankets pulling him down, the wind howling outside. He slept deeply, dreamlessly. When he woke, the storm had broken. The sea was calmer, the sky a pale grey. The light was still burning, steady and warm.

He wrote in his journal. He described the storm, the distress call, the adjustment of the lens. He did not write about the fear, the connection, the weight of responsibility. He wrote about the mechanics of the light, the oil, the rotation. He wrote about the survival of the *Mary Jane*. He did not write about himself.

## Chapter 3: March – The Return of the Sun

March brought a change. The storms eased, the days lengthened, the sun returned, pale but present. The sea softened, the waves smaller, the rocks visible. Elias went out, exploring the island, walking the paths that had been buried under snow and ice. He found the herbs on his windowsill had survived, the mint green and strong, the thyme blooming with small purple flowers. He picked them, drying them in the sun, saving them for later.

The supply boat did not come in March. The weather had been erratic, a mix of storms and clear skies, making the journey too risky. Elias was alone, truly alone. The letters in the drawer remained unopened. The radio was silent. The world was distant, a memory.

He began to explore the island more thoroughly. He walked the perimeter, mapping the rocks, the coves, the cliffs. He found a small cave on the north side, hidden behind a curtain of seaweed. He entered it, the air damp and cold, the walls slick with moisture. He found bones in the sand, small and white, the remains of birds. Seagulls. Crows. The island was named for the crows, but they were rare here, the wind too strong for them to fly easily. The bones were old, bleached by the sun and salt.

He sat in the cave, the sound of the sea echoing off the walls. He felt a strange intimacy with the island, a sense of belonging that he had not felt in years. He was part of the landscape, part of the cycle. He was the keeper, the watcher, the witness. He was not separate from the island. He was embedded in it.

He climbed to the top of the cliff, the highest point on the island. The view was vast, the sea stretching to the horizon, the sky a dome of blue. He saw ships in the distance, tiny specks moving across the water. He saw the mainland, a dark line on the far edge of the world. He felt a longing, a pull, but also a resistance. He did not want to go back. He did not want to be part of the line on the horizon. He wanted to be the point on the rock, the fixed point, the stable center.

He returned to the lighthouse. He wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, cleaned the lens. He ate a meal of dried fish and potatoes. He sat by the window, watching the sun set, the sky turning orange and purple. The light began to burn, the golden beam sweeping the dusk. He watched it, the dance of light and dark, the battle between the sun and the night. He felt a peace, a quiet acceptance. He was where he was supposed to be.

He wrote in his journal. He described the cave, the bones, the view from the cliff. He wrote about the sun, the lengthening days, the return of color. He did not write about the longing, the resistance, the peace. He wrote about the island, the rocks, the birds. He wrote about the mechanics of the light, the oil, the glass. He wrote about the survival of the island, the survival of himself.

## Chapter 4: April – The Visitor

The supply boat came in April, the weather finally stable, the sea calm. Callum arrived with his crates, his red face weathered by the wind, his beard longer. He brought news, letters, and a new supply of oil. He also brought a visitor.

A man stepped off the boat, a young man, perhaps twenty-five, with a camera around his neck and a notebook in his hand. He was a writer, a journalist from a magazine in London, writing an article about lighthouse keepers, about the solitude of the profession, about the romance of the remote. He was eager, bright-eyed, full of questions.

His name was Julian. He wanted to spend a week on the island, observing Elias, learning about his life, interviewing him for the article.

Elias was uncomfortable. The presence of another person was an intrusion, a violation of the silence. He had chosen the island to escape people, to escape conversation, to escape the performance of identity. Julian was a performance, a mask, a demand for explanation.

"You’re Elias Thorne?" Julian asked, his voice too loud for the quiet space.

"Yes," Elias said.

"I’m Julian. I’m writing about lighthouse keepers. I’d like to stay for a week, if you don’t mind."

Elias looked at Callum. Callum shrugged. "He’s harmless. Just wants to see how you live."

Elias nodded. "You can stay. But don’t expect much."

Julian stayed in the small spare room on the second floor, a room that Elias used for storage. He unpacked his camera, his notebook, his books. He asked questions, constantly, relentlessly. "How do you cope with the isolation?" "Do you ever feel lonely?" "What do you think about when you watch the light?" "Do you miss the mainland?"

Elias answered briefly, monosyllabically. "I cope. I am not lonely. I think about the light. I do not miss the mainland."

Julian was not satisfied. He pressed, he probed, he tried to find the story, the emotion, the drama. He wanted a narrative, a journey, a transformation. He wanted Elias to be a character, not a man.

One evening, they sat in the keeper’s quarters, the stove burning, the light sweeping the room. Julian asked, "Why did you come here?"

Elias paused. He looked at the light, the golden beam cutting through the dark. "I came here to watch," he said.

"To watch what?" Julian asked.

"The sea. The light. The time," Elias said.

"That’s not an answer," Julian said.

"It’s the only answer," Elias said.

Julian wrote in his notebook, frowning. He was frustrated, hungry for a story that Elias would not give. He tried again, the next day, while they were checking the lens. "I’ve read about keepers who went mad," he said. "Keepers who talked to the light. Keepers who believed the light was alive. Do you ever feel that?"

Elias looked at the lens, the intricate glass, the complex mechanics. "I feel that the light is important," he said. "I feel that it is necessary. I do not feel that it is alive."

"But you talk to it," Julian said. "I heard you, yesterday. You said, 'Keep burning.' You spoke to the lamp."

Elias looked at him. "I speak to the mechanism," he said. "I check the gears. I check the oil. I speak to the machine."

Julian wrote, his pen scratching loudly. He was writing a story, but it was not the story he wanted. It was a story of silence, of refusal, of a man who would not be understood. He was frustrated, but also fascinated. He was witnessing something real, something raw, something that could not be packaged.

On the third day, Julian went out to the cliff, taking pictures. He photographed Elias, the light, the sea. He asked Elias to pose, to stand by the railing, to look at the horizon. Elias refused. He stood where he was, doing his work, checking the foundation, clearing the ice. He did not perform for the camera. He did not create an image. He was only what he was.

Julian became angry. "You’re making my job impossible," he said. "I need a story. I need a person. I can’t write about a machine."

Elias looked at him. "Then write about the machine," he said. "Write about the light. Write about the sea. Write about the rock. Don’t write about me."

Julian stared at him, his face tight, his eyes burning. He wanted to scream, to demand, to force Elias into a narrative. But Elias was steady, unmoving, unyielding. He was the rock, the stone, the fixed point. He could not be moved.

That night, Julian packed his things. He would leave on the next boat. He had failed to find his story. He had found only silence.

Elias watched him go, the boat shrinking on the horizon. He felt a relief, a release. The intrusion was over. The silence returned. The light swept the room, steady and warm. He was alone again. He was himself again.

He wrote in his journal. He described the visitor, the questions, the refusal. He did not write about Julian’s frustration, his anger, his failure. He wrote about the silence, the steady light, the return of solitude. He wrote about the mechanics of the light, the oil, the glass. He wrote about the survival of the self.

## Chapter 5: May – The Greening

May was a month of color. The sea warmed, the ice melted, the rocks revealed patches of green, moss and lichen spreading like a slow fire. The birds returned, the crows and the seagulls, their cries echoing off the cliffs. The island was alive, a pulse of green and grey and white.

Elias walked the island, exploring the new growth. He found wildflowers in the coves, small and fragile, pushing up through the cracks in the rock. He found the herbs on his windowsill flourishing, the mint and thyme strong, the scent filling the room. He picked them, drying them, saving them for the winter.

He began to feel a connection to the island, a sense of ownership, not of property, but of presence. He was part of the cycle, part of the growth, part of the life. He was not just a keeper. He was a participant.

He went to the cave, the one with the bones. The bones were still there, white and clean. He sat in the cave, the sound of the sea echoing, the light filtering through the entrance, a pale grey glow. He felt a peace, a quiet joy. The island was not hostile. It was not cruel. It was indifferent, but it was also beautiful. It was a place of stark, simple beauty, a beauty that did not demand anything, that did not require performance, that did not require explanation.

He climbed to the top of the cliff. The view was clearer, the horizon sharper. He saw the mainland, the line on the edge of the world. He saw the ships, the specks moving across the water. He felt a connection to them, a thread of light that bound him to the world, but also a distance, a separation. He was on the rock, on the edge, on the boundary. He was the keeper, the watcher, the witness. He was not in the world. He was at the edge of it.

He returned to the lighthouse. He wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, cleaned the lens. He ate a meal of dried fish and potatoes. He sat by the window, watching the sun set, the sky turning orange and purple. The light began to burn, the golden beam sweeping the dusk. He watched it, the dance of light and dark, the battle between the sun and the night. He felt a peace, a quiet acceptance. He was where he was supposed to be.

He wrote in his journal. He described the greenery, the flowers, the birds. He wrote about the cave, the bones, the view from the cliff. He did not write about the peace, the joy, the connection. He wrote about the island, the rocks, the birds. He wrote about the mechanics of the light, the oil, the glass. He wrote about the survival of the island, the survival of himself.

Elias spent the days outside, walking the island, exploring the paths, checking the foundations. He felt strong, his body adapted to the cold, his mind clear. He was not thin anymore. He was lean, hard, weathered. He was part of the rock.

## Chapter 7: July – The Fog

July brought the fog. It came in from the sea, a thick, white wall that swallowed the world. The sea was invisible, the sky a blank, the island a ghost. The light was essential, the only guide in the white void.

Elias stayed inside the tower, watching the lens, ensuring that the beam cut through the fog, that the light was strong, that the mechanism was steady. He felt a tension, a focus. The fog was dangerous, a confusion, a loss of direction. The light was the answer, the solution, the guide.

He went out to check the exterior, the wind calm, the fog thick. He walked the perimeter, the path invisible, the rocks slippery. He used a rope, a safety line, moving slowly, carefully. He checked the foundation, the iron bands, the drainage. He cleared away the damp, the moss, the lichen. He worked with methodical precision, his hands steady, his mind clear. This was the work. This was the purpose. To keep the structure sound. To keep the light burning.

He returned to the tower. He felt a strange sense of closeness, a intimacy with the fog. It was a world of silence, of white, of nothing. It was a world that erased the boundaries, that removed the distinctions. It was a world of pure presence, of pure being.

He sat by the window, watching the fog, the white void. He thought about the ships, the specks moving in the fog, the sailors lost in the white. He felt a connection to them, a thread of light that bound him to the world, but also a distance, a separation. He was the keeper, the watcher, the witness. He was not in the world. ## Chapter 8: August – The Bird

In August, a bird came. It was a cormorant, large and black, with a long neck and sharp eyes. It landed on the balcony, resting, watching the sea. Elias watched it, the bird a presence, a life in the silence.

The bird stayed for a day, then left, flying out over the sea, disappearing into the horizon. Elias felt a loss, a absence, a gap. The bird was a connection to the world, a life, a movement. Its absence was a reminder of the isolation, the solitude, the fixed point.

He went out to the cliff, the highest point on the island. The view was vast, the sea stretching to the horizon, the sky a dome of blue. He saw ships in the distance, tiny specks moving across the water. He saw the mainland, a dark line on the far edge of the world. He felt a longing, a pull, but also a resistance. He did not want to go back. He did not want to be part of the line on the horizon. He wanted to be the point on the rock, the fixed point, the stable center.

## Chapter 10: October – The Letters

In October, the supply boat came. Callum arrived with his crates, his red face weathered by the wind, his beard longer. He brought news, letters, and a new supply of oil. He also brought a package, a small box, wrapped in brown paper.

"This is for you," Callum said, handing him the box. "From your daughter."

Elias took the box. He did not open it. He placed it on the crate, not wanting to break the seal of his isolation. He did not want to know what was happening in London. He did not want to know about Sarah, or the buildings he had designed. He wanted to keep the package as a potential threat, unopened, unspoken.

Callum looked at him. "You should open it, Elias."

"I will," Elias said.

Callum boarded the boat. The engine sputtered, then roared to life. The boat pulled away, cutting through the waves, shrinking to a dot on the horizon. Elias watched it go until it disappeared. Then he turned back to the tower, carrying the crates. The weight of them was heavy, not just in pounds, but in meaning. They were a tether to the world, a reminder that he was not entirely cut off.

He unpacked the supplies. He stacked the canned food, the oil drums, the potatoes. He organized them with care, creating a system that would last him through the winter. He opened the box from Sarah. Inside was a photograph, a picture of her, smiling, holding a baby, a small child with dark hair and bright eyes. On the back, a note: *Dad, this is my son. His name is Leo. I thought you should know.*

Elias looked at the photograph. He looked at the face, the eyes, the smile. He felt a surge of emotion, a pain, a longing, a connection. He looked at the child, the small face, the bright eyes. He felt a love, a fierce, painful love. He looked at the note, the words, the name. *Leo.*

He placed the photograph on the desk, next to the journal. He did not write in the journal. He did not write about the photograph, the note, the love. He wrote about the oil, the wick, the rotation. He wrote about the mechanics of survival. He left the blank space blank.
